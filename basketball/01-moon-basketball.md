# 01 — What if you played basketball on the Moon?

- **Length:** ~60 s, vertical 9:16
- **Language:** English
- **Structure:** 6 stages, each with an on-screen stage card ("STAGE 1 / 6")
- **Look:** 3D, realistic lunar surface, black sky, hard sunlight, Earth on the horizon. A pro player in a white spacesuit with a ball.

---

## Hook (0–3 s)

**VO:** "What if you took a basketball to the Moon? Here's what actually happens."

**Shot:** The camera drops fast from space onto a hoop standing alone on grey lunar dust. Earth is in the background.

**On screen:** WHAT IF… YOU PLAYED BASKETBALL ON THE MOON?

---

## Stage 1 — The ball (3–11 s)

**VO:** "Problem one: your ball. On Earth it's pumped about 8 psi above the air around it. On the Moon there is no air, so it's now pushing out almost three times harder. It swells, and it could burst."

**Shot:** Close-up on the ball as the airlock opens. The ball visibly bulges. Hard shadow.

**On screen:** 8 psi → ~22 psi pressure difference

**Fact check:**
- The NBA ball is inflated to 7.5–8.5 psi (NBA Official Rules, Rule 2).
- Earth's air pressure is ~14.7 psi. In a vacuum it's 0, so the difference becomes ≈ 8 + 14.7 ≈ 22.7 psi, about 2.8× normal. "Could burst" is a hedge: the burst limit depends on the ball.

---

## Stage 2 — The dribble (11–19 s)

**VO:** "You dribble. The ball falls in slow motion, about two and a half times slower than on Earth. And on loose Moon dust it barely comes back up. You need a hard court."

**Shot:** Side view. A split screen with an Earth dribble on the left and a Moon dribble on the right. On the right, the ball hits dust, kicks up a perfect dust arc, and dies.

**On screen:** Earth: 0.45 s · Moon: 1.1 s (1 m drop)

**Fact check:**
- Moon gravity is 1.62 m/s², ≈ 1/6 of Earth's 9.81 (NASA Moon Fact Sheet).
- Fall time is t = √(2h/g). A 1 m drop takes 0.45 s on Earth and 1.11 s on the Moon. The ratio is √6 ≈ 2.45.
- Lunar regolith is loose and powdery (Apollo surface reports), so a bounce on it loses most of its energy. This is qualitative, not a measured number.
- **Visual accuracy note:** with no air, dust flies in clean arcs and falls straight back down. It does NOT hang as a cloud. Show it like that.

---

## Stage 3 — The shot (19–29 s)

**VO:** "You shoot a normal free throw, and it flies about 29 metres. That's the length of an NBA court. On the Moon, a free throw is a gentle toss. And there's no air, so no backspin curve and no swish. Total silence."

**Shot:** The ball leaves his hands, sails over the backboard, and the camera follows it for a long time. Then a second attempt: a soft underhand toss drops through the net. No sound at all, and the music cuts out.

**On screen:** Normal shot = ~29 m · Needed: ~2.8 m/s instead of ~7 m/s

**Fact check:**
- Projectile range is R = v²·sin(2θ)/g. A typical free throw is ~7 m/s at ~52°. On the Moon that gives ≈ 29 m. This is simplified: it uses flat ground and ignores release height.
- The NBA court is 94 ft ≈ 28.65 m.
- The speed needed scales with √g, so 7 / 2.45 ≈ 2.8 m/s.
- Sound needs a medium, and a vacuum carries none. No air also means no drag and no Magnus effect (the spin curve).

---

## Stage 4 — The jump (29–41 s)

**VO:** "Now the jump. Take an NBA-average 28-inch vertical. Without a suit, you'd rise over four metres and stay in the air for four and a half seconds. But you're wearing about 80 kilos of spacesuit, so it's more like three metres. That's still enough to put your head above the rim."

**Shot:** He crouches and launches. A slow orbit camera circles him at the top, and a hang-time counter ticks up: 1… 2… 3… 4. His head is above the rim.

**On screen:** Earth hang time: 0.76 s · Moon (suited): ~4 s

**Fact check:**
- 28 in ≈ 0.71 m vertical gives a takeoff speed of 3.73 m/s.
  - No suit on the Moon: h = v²/2g = 4.3 m, hang time = 2v/g = 4.6 s.
  - **Estimate**, assuming a 100 kg player, an ~80 kg suit and backpack, the same leg work, and 0.4 m of push: ~3.3 m rise and ~4.1 s hang time. The real figure would be lower, because a pressurised suit resists bending the knees.
- The Apollo EMU suit plus life-support pack weighed roughly 80–90 kg on Earth (NASA). That's why Apollo astronauts hopped rather than ran.
- Unchanged: your mass and inertia. Landing feels normal, because you come down at the same speed you left.

---

## Stage 5 — The dunk (41–50 s)

**VO:** "So can you dunk? Physically, easily. The real problem is the suit. Apollo suits made it hard to lift your arms above your head. Your body could dunk. Your suit might not let you."

**Shot:** Floating at the top of the jump, his arm strains to rise against the stiff suit. Cut to black as the ball is about to touch the rim.

**Fact check:**
- Apollo pressure suits were pressurised to ~3.7 psi, which made joints stiff and limited reach (NASA Apollo EMU documentation). Treat this as qualitative.

---

## Stage 6 — The ending / loop (50–60 s)

**VO:** "And if you took the helmet off to celebrate? You'd have about fifteen seconds before you passed out. So on the Moon the hardest part isn't the dunk. It's staying alive long enough to take the shot."

**Shot:** The visor reflects Earth. The camera pulls back to the lone hoop, the same framing as the opening shot (loop point).

**On screen:** ~15 s of useful consciousness in a vacuum

**Fact check:**
- NASA says useful consciousness in a vacuum lasts roughly 10–15 s. The body does not explode. Skin and tissue swell, and saliva boils (ebullism).
